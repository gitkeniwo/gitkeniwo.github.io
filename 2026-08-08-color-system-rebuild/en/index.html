Rebuilding the colour system: from hand-tuned hex to a ramp you can solve for

[ 中文 ] [ English ]
ケニヲ
2026-08-08
Zen and the Art of Computer Maintenance › Programming Languages
design , css , color , notes

This site has two themes: pink in light, terminal green in dark. This is the record of moving them from a pile of hand-tuned hex codes to a ramp that can be computed — the trigger, the measurements, the options that got rejected, and the structure that shipped.

Every swatch, ladder and contrast table lives on /design/colors/en/, which resolves them at build time in OKLCH and parses the “today” column straight out of variables.css. The numbers here are quoted for the argument; that page is the authority. I deliberately did not copy the tables into this post — copy them once and there will be a first day they disagree.

1. The trigger: it was never the page

The original complaint was specific: the /irc/ channel page looked cluttered and misaligned.

But the cause was not that page’s layout — it was the variable layer. The channel page just happened to be where the palette’s defects were most visible. So the palette got fixed first, and the typography after.

2. Four things we measured

Every decision below rests on a measurement rather than on “that looks off”. Backgrounds are #e2e0de light and #1d1f21 dark; cr is the WCAG contrast ratio (1 = invisible, 21 = black on white, body text needs ≥ 4.5).

1. The light theme was under-contrast across the board. link 3.28, strong 2.06, em 2.73, toc-active 2.39 — nearly everything below the AA floor of 4.5, while the dark side sat steadily around 7. The two themes were not stylistically different; one was readable and one was not.

2. Three nearly identical pinks were fighting. #d44375 (23 uses), #c04059 (11), #c73866 (3): 0.04 apart in lightness, 0.02 in chroma, 10° in hue. Close enough that the eye insists they should be the same colour, not close enough that they are. “Almost the same” reads messier than plainly different — that was the channel page’s clutter.

3. The muted layer really was brown. #8dba8a, the dark theme’s secondary, is not a quiet green: its hue is 21° yellower than the family and it uses a third of the chroma available at that lightness. Olive next to terminal green was never going to sit right.

4. The bright pink was not using its gamut. #d44375 spends only 76% of the chroma available at its lightness. Pushed to the edge it gets more saturated and more legible at once — meaning readability never required trading the pink for a wine red.

3. The hue-locked ladder

The fourth measurement is the foundation for everything after it: lock the hue, keep the chroma, move only lightness.

Desaturating to make a “quiet layer” had already been rejected on sight — low-chroma pink and green read as brown and khaki, and they leave a hole between themselves and the accent. On a hue-locked ladder, every stop is still pink, still green, with no discontinuity in between.

One thing that comes up repeatedly below: “receding” points in opposite directions per theme. On a dark ground, darker means less contrast, which recedes; on a light ground, darker means more contrast, which advances. That is the background’s doing and there is no way around it.

4. The second voice: four candidates, one rule

The first round moved exactly one role — the “second voice” (banner status, [#chan], the day-change date, the active tab mark) — leaving the primary untouched so the variable was isolated. All four candidates come off the ladder:

CandidateΔLDirectioncr ×Verdict
F light #c51963−0.06away from bg×1.32✓ chosen
E dark #2ad69c+0.07away from bg×1.29✓ chosen

(Primaries: #d44375 light, cr 3.28 · #2bbc8a dark, cr 6.82)

The two that won are not “one nice colour picked per theme”. They are the same rule executed twice: the second voice is the primary, moved one rung away from the background. The step sizes are near-identical (×1.32 vs ×1.29). What is conserved is the relationship inside each theme, not the numbers across them — the light theme is not an inversion of the dark one.

The two rejects are the same mistake. E light gets brighter and D dark gets darker, which looks like opposite moves, but in the coordinate that actually matters — distance from the background — they are the same move: both toward the background, both about two rungs, both washing out.

D light’s wine red had the right direction and the wrong step size: two rungs in one jump, with nothing in between. It was not discarded; see the next section.

5. One rung is not enough — you need a ramp

Choosing F for light exposed a contradiction. Solve for “the first pink on this hue that clears AA 4.5” and the answer is F itself. Which means the second voice is now more legible than the lead — the premise that the primary stays put no longer holds.

The fix is to stop assigning roles by importance and assign them by text size instead: large text needs 3.0, body text needs 4.5.

The loud pink keeps “what you notice first” on the headings and ASCII art, where the area is largest and the bar is lowest; legibility lives in the body rung. The two requirements stop fighting over one token. The wine red lands on hover, a full rung below body, so the jump problem dissolves on its own.

Every rung is solved backwards from a target contrast ratio, no hex hand-tuned — and the two themes’ targets are deliberately unequal: in the dark theme the loudest rung has more contrast than the body rung, and in the light theme it has less. That inversion is not a slip. On a light ground the loudest pink is the one closest to the background; on a dark ground the brightest green is the one furthest from it. Loudness costs something different in each.

Incidentally, the light theme’s loudest pink is #e21e73. The closest conventional colour name is Razzmatazz (#e3256b, Crayola 1993) — 0.015 apart in OKLab, effectively indistinguishable — followed by Rosa Mexicano #e4007c and Ruby #e0115f. Its chroma of 0.228 beats the shipped #d44375 (0.184): more saturated and more legible.

6. Roles reshuffled

The semantic layer contains no numbers at all, only rung references:

--color-link:       var(--a-base);   /* body size → the AA rung */
--color-link-hover: var(--a-deep);
--color-accent-1:   var(--a-vivid);  /* headings, ASCII → the loud rung */
--color-meta:       var(--n-quiet);  /* today a theme-blind #666 */

Because the semantic names do not change, most of the 289 var(--color-*) call sites across the site need no edit. The real cost is re-reading every page in the light theme.

Three historical defects got fixed on the way past:

  • --color-meta and --color-border were a theme-blind #666 (4.36 on light, 2.88 on dark) — which is why the IRC prefix column behaves oppositely per theme.
  • --color-quote differed by 6× in chroma between themes.
  • The dark theme’s glow was cyan, rgba(0,255,255,…), a different family from the site’s green entirely.

The full token table is on /design/colors/en/; red marks body-size tokens below 4.5, and today’s light column is almost entirely red.

7. Swatches lie

The same colour in 12px meta text and across a block of ASCII art are not the same experience. So the last step is putting the candidates back onto real interfaces: the IRC channel, the home page, a post body — each rendered with real components and real data, today stacked directly above the proposal.

Stacked rather than side by side, because two half-width columns force the IRC prefix column and the ls tags/ grid to wrap at widths the real site never sees, which makes the comparison about reflow instead of about colour.

8. How this takes another theme

The whole thing has two data sources, both pure functions with no dependencies:

FileContents
scripts/oklch.mjsOKLCH ↔ sRGB, gamut ceiling, WCAG contrast, solveAgainst(target, hue, bg)
scripts/theme.mjsTHEMES (a theme = background + hue + contrast targets), ROLES (token → rung)

Adding a theme is one entry in THEMES — a background, a hue and a set of target ratios, no hex codes. The ladder, the rungs, the swatches, the comparison table and every specimen gain a copy automatically.

solveAgainst picks its search direction from the background’s luminance rather than from a theme name, so two dark themes on different surfaces resolve to different ramps instead of sharing one.

node scripts/palette.mjs rungs prints the same data in a terminal.

The method reduces to one sentence: measure first, then change. “That looks off” is a real signal, but it does not tell you what is off — “these three pinks are 0.04 apart in lightness” does.
